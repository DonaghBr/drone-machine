Drone Machine

Build a tune with the beat pads, sequencer and note strip, then bring in the drone whenever you like. Stuck? Hit Randomise.

📘 New here? Learn the machine

Your first sound in a minute

The machine has three kinds of parts. Holding sounds (the drone) give atmosphere. Pattern players (beat pads, sequencer, arpeggio) repeat on their own. Hand-played parts (keyboard, note strip, swipe pads) are for you. Everything follows one key and scale, so it’s hard to play a wrong note.

  1. Press Start drone. A slow chord fades in.
  2. Tap a note letter, then pick a scale.
  3. Move the five Sound sliders and listen to what each one does.
  4. Drag a finger around the swipe pads.
  5. Play the keyboard. Dotted keys belong to your scale.
  6. Stuck? Press Randomise. Love it? Save it as a preset.
💡 Good to knowEverything saves automatically on this device. Reset returns the sound to its preset without touching your beats.
🎧 Listening tipUse headphones or proper speakers. Phone speakers can’t play the deep bass and sub notes, so half the music disappears.

Build a tune in layers

Most music is a few layers that come and go. Think of four jobs: rhythm, bass, melody and atmosphere. Give each job to one part of the machine.

  1. Rhythm. In Beat pads, pick a kit and press Start pattern.
  2. Bass. Press Start sequencer and try a few Starters.
  3. Melody. Play the keyboard, or press Start arpeggio.
  4. Atmosphere. Start the drone and choose how it behaves: held, swells, pumps or pulses.
  5. Capture. In the Looper, press Rec. It records 2 bars exactly on the beat. Then use Overdub to add layers.
  6. Keep it. Press Record audio to save a sound file, or save a preset.
🧱 Arrange in 8-bar blocksStart with one part. Add a part every 8 bars. Take one away near the end so the return feels big.
🎚 Make spaceIf it sounds muddy, two parts are fighting over the same range. Turn Brightness down, or play the keyboard an octave higher.

Neu! and the motorik beat

📻 The storyNeu! was a Düsseldorf duo, Klaus Dinger and Michael Rother, who had both played in an early Kraftwerk line-up in 1971. Dinger’s drumming was a steady, unbroken 4/4 he called the “Apache beat”, now known as motorik. It feels like driving down an endless road. Rother layered bright, chiming guitar over it. Listen to: “Hallogallo” (1972).

The recipe: one chord, a beat that never changes, and a slow brightening over several minutes.

  1. Press Set it up for me, then Start pattern. Let it run without touching it.
  2. After 8 bars, press Start sequencer. The bass pulses on the root note.
  3. Press Start drone. It swells in and out every 4 bars.
  4. On the keyboard, hold long notes: D, then A, then F♯. Change only every few bars.
  5. Over two minutes, slide Brightness up very slowly. That gradual lift is the whole song.
🔁 Repetition is the pointMotorik tracks can sit on one chord for ten minutes. Change the texture, not the chords.
🎵 Why Mixolydian?It’s a major scale with a lowered 7th note. It sounds bright but never quite resolves, which suits a groove that never ends.
🎸 Rother’s trickBuild a part from one short phrase played over and over. Record a phrase with Rec tune, loop it, and slowly change the lead Tone in advanced mode. Small changes in tone keep it alive.

Can: the locked groove

📻 The storyCan formed in Cologne in 1968: Holger Czukay (bass), Irmin Schmidt (keyboards), Michael Karoli (guitar) and Jaki Liebezeit (drums), with singers Malcolm Mooney and later Damo Suzuki. They improvised for hours, and Czukay edited the tapes into finished tracks. Liebezeit played tight, repeating grooves with almost no fills, funky and machine-like at once. Listen to: “Halleluhwah” (1971) and “Vitamin C” (1972).

The recipe: a locked, slightly swung groove, a minor riff that loops, and raw organ stabs with lots of gaps.

  1. Press Set it up for me, then start the pattern and the sequencer together.
  2. On the keyboard (set to Organ), play short stabs on A, C and E with Sustain off. Leave plenty of silence between them.
  3. Raise Grit a little for a raw, overdriven tone.
  4. Press Record audio and jam for five minutes. Afterwards, keep only the best part, the way Can did.
  5. Use the Looper’s Overdub to add a scratch or a second riff.
🥁 The Liebezeit ruleOnce the groove feels good, don’t add fills. Keep the pattern steady and let the other parts move around it.
🎵 Why Dorian?It’s a minor scale with a raised 6th note. It sounds minor but not sad, which is why it’s the classic scale for funk and jamming.

Brian Eno and ambient music

📻 The storyBrian Eno named “ambient music” with Ambient 1: Music for Airports (1978). He wanted music you could ignore or listen to closely, and both would work. On Discreet Music (1975) he set up a system of tape delays and let it play itself, an early example of generative music. For Music for Airports he used tape loops of different lengths, so the notes kept meeting in new combinations.

The recipe: few notes, lots of space, long fades, and loops that never quite line up.

  1. Press Set it up for me, then Start drone. Notice how slowly it fades in.
  2. Press Start sequencer. A few soft notes, far apart.
  3. Press Rec tune and play three or four notes slowly on the note strip. Stop at an odd length, like 7 seconds, then press Play with Loop playback on.
  4. Sustain is on for the keyboard. Add an occasional single note and let it ring.
  5. Now stop touching it. Let the machine play itself for a few minutes.
🤫 Silence is an instrumentIf in doubt, play fewer notes. Ambient music lives in the space between sounds.
🎵 Why pentatonic?Five notes with no clashing pairs, so any combination sounds calm. Perfect when the machine is choosing the combinations for you.
🃏 Make your own prompt cardsEno and the artist Peter Schmidt made a card deck called Oblique Strategies, with short prompts to break creative blocks. Make your own: “use only three notes”, “remove the loudest part”, “play it twice as slow”.

Practice challenges

Short exercises that build real musical habits. Try one a day.

  1. One-chord tune. Make a whole piece that never leaves the root note. Keep it interesting with texture alone.
  2. The 4-bar rule. Every 4 bars, change exactly one thing. Not two.
  3. Same song, new genre. Keep your sequencer and drone, and switch the kit from Boom bap to Synth drums.
  4. Ten-minute drone. Set the drone to Swells, start a timer, and only touch Brightness.
  5. Mood swap. Load a preset you like, change only the scale, and listen to the mood shift.
👂 Listen like a producerPlay a favourite track and name the four jobs: what’s the rhythm, the bass, the melody, the atmosphere? Then build those four here.
💾 Keep your experimentsSave a ★ preset whenever something sounds good. Small happy accidents are hard to find twice.
